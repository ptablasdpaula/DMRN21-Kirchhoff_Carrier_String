\begin{abstract}
Although realistic, finite-difference time-domain schemes are a rare choice
in differentiable sound synthesis due to their slow and memory-hungry
backpropagation. We compare ways to reduce this cost for a string whose
tension rises as it vibrates, the Kirchhoff--Carrier model. We propose a
method that steps through the string's modes in parallel, with a backward
pass derived by hand for the GPU, and show it is faster and lighter than
automatic differentiation, even when compiled and checkpointed. An online
supplement applies it to sound matching of recorded guitar and
text-guided sound design.\footnotemark
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) sound synthesis is increasingly lean~\cite{bilbao2024guitar} and runs on the GPU~\cite{lee2024string}, yet differentiable
frameworks~\cite{hayes2024review} largely avoid it because
backpropagating through a time-stepping scheme builds deep recurrent graphs. Hence, the literature has favoured
digital waveguides~\cite{tablas2026sound} and modal
synthesis~\cite{diaz2025}. Physics simulation has long met the same obstacle
with the adjoint method and checkpointing, now the default of
differential-equation solvers~\cite{kidger2021}.
We apply this recipe to a nonlinear FDTD string, propose stepping through
its modes in parallel via a sine transform, and measure what each
ingredient buys in PyTorch\footnotemark{} and in JAX.\footnotemark

\section{Model and method}
\head{Kirchhoff--Carrier string}
The Kirchhoff--Carrier string~\cite[Sec.~8.1]{bilbao2009} is stiff and lossy,
and its tension rises with its stretch. Simply supported at both
ends~\cite{russo2026}, it obeys
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\rho A\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial t^2}={}&
 \bigg[T_0+\frac{EA}{2L}\!\int_0^L\!\Big(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\Big)^{\!2}dx\bigg]
 \frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}
 -EI\frac{\partial^4u}{\partial x^4}\\
 &-2\rho A\Big(\sigma_0-\sigma_1\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\Big)
 \frac{\partial u}{\partial t},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pde}
\end{equation}
with displacement $u(x,t)$, density $\rho$, cross-section $A$, Young's
modulus $E$, second moment of area $I$, length $L$ and damping
$\sigma_0,\sigma_1$. The bracket is the tension: its rest value $T_0$ plus a
term that grows with the string's stretch, one number for the whole string,
which is what bends the pitch of a hard pluck. We integrate
(\ref{eq:pde}) with the energy-conserving scheme of Russo \emph{et
al.}~\cite{russo2026}. A fourth-order five-point
stencil~\cite[Sec.~6.3.1]{bilbao2009} reduces
numerical dispersion.

\head{Backpropagation}
We compare nine implementations in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}, in PyTorch
and in JAX, each adding one ingredient to the row above. \emph{Auto} is the
scheme's adjoint, derived by automatic differentiation. Every state is stored, and
every operation of every step incurs the overhead of a Python call;
\emph{+~compiled} compiles blocks of 16 unrolled steps in PyTorch, and the
scheme and its gradient as one program in JAX. \emph{+~checkpoints} stores the state only
periodically and re-runs one segment at a time on the reverse pass.
\emph{+~modes} applies a discrete sine transform, after which each stencil
becomes one multiplication per mode and the modes interact only through the
nonlinear tension term. \emph{Hand-written} is the same adjoint with the
same checkpoints and modes, derived by hand and written as a single GPU
(CUDA\footnotemark) kernel, parallel over modes and batch items. All nine return the
gradient of the discrete scheme.

\footnotetext[1]{\fontsize{6}{7}\selectfont\url{ptablasdpaula.github.io/DMRN21-Kirchhoff_Carrier_String}}%
\footnotetext[2]{\fontsize{6}{7}\selectfont\url{pytorch.org}\quad\inlinemark{3}\url{docs.jax.dev}\quad\inlinemark{4}\url{developer.nvidia.com/cuda}}%
Each evaluation is one sound matching step: a batch of strings is simulated
for 1 s, the mean absolute error of each waveform against the target's is backpropagated to ten parameters:
$\rho$, $T_0$, $r$ ($A=\pi r^2$, $I=\pi r^4/4$), $E$, $L$, $\sigma_0$,
$\sigma_1$, the pluck amplitude and the pluck and pickup positions. The target
is a steel string ($\rho=8050$ kg/m$^3$, $T_0=75$ N, $r=0.356$ mm, $E=174$ GPa,
$L=1$ m, $\sigma_0=0.92$ s$^{-1}$, $\sigma_1=2.9\times10^{-4}$ m$^2$/s), plucked
at its centre with a 2 mm raised cosine of half-width $0.24L$, its
displacement read at its midpoint; the batch perturbs every parameter by up to
$\pm3\%$. The scheme divides the string into 176 equal segments and runs
at 88.2 kHz, decimated to 44.1 kHz, on an A100 80 GB in float64, with a
checkpoint every 256 steps. In Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}, memory
grows linearly with the batch without checkpoints, nearing or exceeding the
A100\textquotesingle s 80\,GB, and stays around 2\,GB or less with them.
Compiling cuts PyTorch\textquotesingle s time by an order of magnitude and
JAX\textquotesingle s by three, making JAX the faster; mode-stepping cuts both further. Our kernel is faster and lighter than the fastest of them.
